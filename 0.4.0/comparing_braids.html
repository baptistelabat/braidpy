Comparing braids

A braid made on a machine, a braid typed as a word and a braid read off a kumihimo pattern can be the same braid and look nothing alike on paper. The same braid can be written from another starting point, with far crossings swapped, made of more or fewer repeats, its strands numbered from the other side. To say whether two braids are alike — and to suggest, for a braid of your own, the braids it is most like — the comparison has to be about the braid’s topology, not about how it happens to have been written.

braidpy.similarity does so with a fingerprint: a few numbers that do not change however the braid is written, and a distance between fingerprints.

from braidpy import Braid
from braidpy.similarity import similar

# The plait, typed from its second crossing and repeated five times.
mine = Braid([-2, 1] * 5, 3)
similar(mine, count=3)
# [Match(name='flat3', distance=0.0, same_fingerprint=True), ...]

Four kinds of “the same”

“The same braid” can mean four things, each coarser than a word, and braidpy gives each its own type, its own == and its own hash, so that a set or a dict of them keeps one of each:

A pattern and a closed braid are both coarser than a braid, but neither is coarser than the other: the plait and the plait twice are one pattern and close into two knots (a plain loop, the figure-eight knot); σ₁ on two strands and a single strand close into one loop and are two patterns. Grommets and Turk’s heads are closed braids.

from braidpy import Braid

a, b = Braid([1, 2], 3), Braid([2, 1], 3)
a == b  # False: two braids
a.pattern() == b.pattern()  # True: one read from the other's second crossing
Braid([1], 2).closed() == Braid([], 1).closed()  # True: one loop

Deciding it exactly

“The same braid, read from another starting point” is conjugacy in the braid group, and braidpy.conjugacy decides it for patterns: same_pattern() (once called same_braid, a name kept) says yes or no, a proof, where fingerprints only ever prove braids different.

from braidpy.braid import Braid
from braidpy.conjugacy import same_pattern

same_pattern(Braid([1, -2], 3), Braid([-2, 1], 3))  # True

It goes by summit sets (Elrifai and Morton https://nsalter.science.nd.edu/teaching/braidsspring2024/elrifaimorton.pdf): each braid is cycled to its super summit set, the conjugates as short as any in Garside’s normal form, and that finite set is searched whole; two braids are conjugate exactly when their sets meet. A first implementation of that conjugated by every permutation braid, n! of them, and took minutes on 6 and 8 strands. This one follows only the least permutation braids that keep a braid in the set, at most one for each crossing σᵢ (Franco and González-Meneses): edo yatsu’s set, 472 braids on 8 strands, is found in about 3 seconds. The set can still grow exponentially with the strands, so the search stops at a limit and says so (SuperSummitSetTooLarge).

A yes or no does not suggest braids like yours: for that, a distance is wanted, and the fingerprint gives one.

The fingerprint

It is of the braid’s pattern, not its length: the word’s repeating unit, and that unit repeated until every strand is back where it started — the home braid, whose closure makes a loop of each strand.

None of them changes when the braid is read from another starting point, and none but the writhe when its strands are numbered from the other side. The distance adds a share from 0 to 1 for each, 0 for the same fingerprint.

Why the Alexander polynomial alone is not enough

The Alexander polynomial Δ(t) of a braid’s closure is one of the strongest of the six, and it is computed from the word through the Burau matrix (braidpy.properties.alexander_polynomial()). But on its own:

  • It does not tell a braid from its mirror image: a rope laid S from one laid Z. The writhe does.

  • It loses the braid itself: it is an invariant of the closure, the same for braids on different numbers of strands that close up alike (Markov’s theorem). The number of strands and the loops keep that.

  • It vanishes for loops that could be pulled apart, and for some links that cannot: then it says nothing. Linking numbers still do.

  • No handful of invariants is complete: different links can share one.

Linking numbers alone are not enough either: the plait and three strands hanging straight both have all their linking numbers 0, and the Alexander polynomial tells them apart.

The Burau polynomial, where the Alexander polynomial vanishes

The Alexander polynomial of a closed braid is det(1 − B), B the braid’s Burau matrix, up to a factor: the characteristic polynomial det(x − B) of the matrix at x = 1. Where B has the eigenvalue 1, it is 0, as for many braids whose closure makes several loops, and it tells nothing apart. The whole characteristic polynomial does not vanish; like the Alexander polynomial, it does not change when the braid is read from another starting point. Sampled at x = 2, on the same circle (braidpy.properties.burau_polynomial_on_circle()), it tells apart braids that agree on everything else:

Before the Burau polynomial was in the fingerprint, these were catalogued as the same braids. It is computed from the eigenvalues of the unit’s Burau matrix, raised to the number of repeats, so that a home braid of thousands of crossings overflows nothing.

The Alexander polynomial, sampled

The home braid of a long sinnet is long: ABOK #3048’s 85-crossing unit repeated 60 times. Its polynomial, expanded, takes minutes. Its modulus on the unit circle, |Δ(e^{iθ})|, is exactly an invariant — on the circle |±tᵏ| = 1, nothing is left to normalise — and is computed numerically from the unit’s Burau matrix at t = e^{iθ}, raised to the number of repeats (braidpy.properties.alexander_on_circle()): milliseconds. It matches the polynomial, where both can be computed, to six digits.

A catalogue by topology

braidpy.topology_catalog lists braids by what they are rather than by how they are made. It makes every braid of every other catalogue — braid words, kumihimo, kumihimo disks, sinnets, braiding machines — and groups those with the same fingerprint. Each topology points to every way of making it, and each way back to its topology (topology_of()):

from braidpy.topology_catalog import topology_of

plait = topology_of("machine", "flat_3")
[m.key for m in plait.methods]
# ['word:flat3', 'word:inverted_flat3', 'sinnet:abok_2965', 'machine:flat_3']

The plait is a braid word, the Dutch braid (the plait seen from behind), Ashley’s #2965 worked on his stand, and what a three-carrier flat braiding machine makes. For a braid of your own, related() gives the topologies nearest it, and with each the ways to make it.

A disk braid that creeps round the disk from cycle to cycle, as kongo gumi does, is fingerprinted over as many cycles as bring every strand home, not its first. A machine braiding round cores counts each core as a strand of its word, one standing still, which the others pass in front of or behind. Making every braid takes some ten seconds, so the catalogue is built once, when first asked for.

Braid Studio shows it beside every braid it makes: Also made as, the other ways of making the same braid, and Nearest braids. The catalogue is made with the site, as JSON (export()), and read back in the browser (load()); for_page() finds a catalogue’s braid by its description, and any other by the fingerprint of the braid made.

Limits

  • Equal fingerprints do not prove two braids the same. Different ones prove them different; same_pattern() proves either.

  • A braid seen from behind is the same braid. The Dutch braid, σ₁⁻¹σ₂, is the plait σ₁σ₂⁻¹ with its strands numbered from the other side, and has its fingerprint.

  • The distance’s weights are a choice: each part counts the same.
